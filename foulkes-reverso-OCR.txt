‘When the French set out to make the show in Paris,
to which this Summer they invited the world, they
announced that everything must be new. The trammels
of tradition were to be removed and art was to be free

in experiment in the service of a civilisation which, in the
article of mechanics, is so radically changed since most
of the traditions of art were invented. The idea has been
carried out with French thoroughness. Consequently,
whatever else the visitor to the Exposition of Decorative
Arts gets, he does not fail to get a shock. It may be a
thrill of pleasure or an emotion of horror—or very likely
a mixture of the two’.'

So wrote The New York Times in 1925 of the event
that would come to define aesthetic culture in the years
between the World Wars. This event would give its name
to an entire global movement, which would be defined
by its chief scholar as ‘an assertively modern style,
developing in the 1920s and reaching its high point
in the 1930s; it drew inspiration from various sources,
including the more austere side of Art Nouveau, Cubism,
the Russian Ballet, Native American art and the Bauhaus;
it was a classical style in that, like neoclassicism but

unlike Rococo or Art Nouveau, it ran to symmetry rather
than asymmetry, and to the rectilinear rather than the
curvilinear; it responded to the demands of the machine
and of new materials such as plastics, ferro-concrete and
vita-glass; and its ultimate aim was to end the old conflict
between art and industry, the old snobbish distinction
between artist and artisan, partly by making artists
adept at crafts, but still more by adapting design to the
demands of mass production’?

Even though the term ‘Art Deco’ would not emerge
for over forty years, it was clear to all sixteen million who
visited it at the time that the Exposition Internationale
des Arts Décoratifs et Industriels Modernes that opened
in the center of Paris in April 1925 was a grand exhibition
of immense cultural significance.

Since 1851, when Joseph Paxton’s iron-and-glass
palace, towering over the trees of London's Hyde Park,
had welcomed the world to the Great Exhibition of the
Works of Industry of All Nations, the concept of the
international exposition had established itself as the
premier international showcase for industrial and artistic
development and an effective instrument of soft power.

The mention of the watchcase as an exemplar of
modernity alongside ‘art furniture’ and architecture shows
just how central the personal timepiece—which by 1925
increasingly meant the wristwatch—was to the vision of the
modern world. As change moved ever more rapidly, a person
needed to keep track of every fleeting moment. The leisurely
elegance of pulling a watch from a pocket and consulting
its dial was replaced by the contemporary and streamlined
gesture of a glance at the wrist.

Like the aircraft, the wristwatch had emerged out of
nowhere at the beginning of the century to eclipse the age-
old hegemony of the pocket watch. Well, not quite absolutely
nowhere. Wrist-worn watches had existed before, more
usually as women’s watches. Notable examples include one
made by Breguet for Caroline Murat, the Queen of Naples,
and the watch set into a gold bangle by Patek Philippe for the Countess Koscowicz in
1868. By the late nineteenth century, wristwatches were accepted as pieces of jewellery
alongside brooch and pendant watches: items of value with dual functions, ornamental
and horological. The permanent collection at Jaeger-LeCoultre holds many beautiful

examples of brooch and pendant watches from this period, embellished with a variety
of decorative techniques, including bas relief sculpting, gem setting, engraving, and
enamelling. At the same time, LeCoultre was also pioneering feminine wristwatches,
and one of the oldest pieces in the Manufacture's collection is an extremely early wrist-
watch, dating from the 1890s, set with forty-six diamonds and twenty rubies, mounted
on a slender gold bangle.

It was only in military circles that the wristwatch was worn by men. In the 1880s,
Girard Perregaux is said to have received an order for wrist-worn timepieces. Pocket
and pendant watches were adapted for wear on the wrist and described as ‘campaign’
watches. The London firm of Mappin & Webb claims they were ‘first used in great
Numbers at Omdurman', an 1898 battle that marked a turning point in the Mahdist War,
and they appeared during the Boer War.

Given the highly innovative character of the Reverso, it
is easy to see it as the product of its times; however, to
understand it only as an ingenious creation of the age of
self-conscious neophilia and unfettered progress would
be at the expense of the culture of technical innovation
that had prevailed at LeCoultre in Switzerland's Vallée de
Joux for over a century before the Reverso made its debut.

By the early part of the nineteenth century, the
Industrial Revolution reached the Vallée de Joux in the
form of watchmaking, stemming from the area's long
tradition of iron-working, which had developed thanks
to iron ore and a plentiful supply of timber to fuel the
forges of the region's blacksmiths.

One such forge was owned by Jacques-David
LeCoultre, and he was soon joined at the anvil by his eldest

son Charles-Antoine (known as Antoine), who already

at the age of 11 was proving himself a skilled artisan,
Crafting blades and penknives for the sharpening of
goose quills, which were the prestige writing instruments
f the day. By the time he was 16 he had graduated to
Naking tuned metal combs for music boxes, and from

€re moved on to the manufacture of precision tools.

Keen to expand his technical knowledge further, in
the summer of 1828 he left his tranquil mountain home
for Geneva, where he would study at the newly founded
school of watchmaking. Not unlike the events a century
and a half later on the other side of the world in Silicon
Valley, the Vallée de Joux was becoming a centre of a
new high-tech industry, of which the ambitious 25-year-
old wanted to be a part. He studied technical drawing,
chemistry, mathematics, and physics. Although he barely
spent a year at the school, he returned home fascinated
by gears and escapements, and the summer of 1829
saw him commence his first watchmaking forays, in a

workshop that still made knives and razor blades.

At that time, watch components were made by
hand, and the concomitant irregularities necessitated
painstaking adjustment of each part by the watchmaker.
Pinions, for example, were notoriously hard to make:
These minute toothed components are among the
foundations of any mechanical movement; yet in the
late 1820s, the quality of imprecisely shaped pinions
fashioned from soft steel was lamentable. Immediately
grasping the significance of these parts, Antoine set

the Banque Cantonale Vaudoise, the business was restructured as a limited company,

remain a director, he was joined by experienced businessman, army colonel and ‘man

Borgeaud & Cie, which also benefitted from the oversight of a comité de surveillance

The application of counter-enamel is equivalent to an
artist preparing a canvas before applying the first brushstrokes

the caseback, each one indi idually fired. After each application of a single colour of
enamel paint, a suspension of a metal oxide, the caseback is once more placed ina

painstaking process fixes the image, which then needs to be protected with ten further

bubbles may appear, and the intensity of certain colours may degrade, thus ruining
hours of work.

a jewelled-up man’s watch but a mechanical timepiece
conceived, designed and made with women in mind...
and it is a Reverso.

In addition to breaking the taboo surrounding
women and mechanical watches, the enduring success
of the Reverso Duetto and the numbers in which it sold
enabled the Manufacture to start building a coherent
collection for women that is founded on the appeal of
the whimsical double-face watch. This is a significant
milestone in the journey of the modern Reverso in that
the Duetto reestablished the Reverso as a watch for
women, just as it had been in the 1930s.

In 2016, the Manufacture began to introduce a
quick-change mechanism for the watch strap that revolu-
tionised the way the Reverso is worn: the versatility
already offered by the Duetto concept is exponentia ly
increased with a range of straps and bracelets in colours

and materials only limited by the wearer's imagination
or daring.
